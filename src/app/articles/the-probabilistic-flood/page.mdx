A UK flood map is still usually a 1-in-100 outline. That was a reasonable product when a model run took a long time and a forecast was a warning symbol. The question we can now ask is narrower: given this storm, on this ground, what is the chance this building is wet?

![Storm to CEFA hydrograph band to Tiamat depth map to probability a building floods](/images/articles/the-probabilistic-flood/01-handshake.jpg)

[CEFA](/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape) (Catchment-Event Frequency Analysis) estimates the hydrograph as a range. [Tiamat](/articles/tiamat) spreads each member of that range across the terrain. The fraction of members that wet a cell is a probability. It is a count of computed floods, not a qualitative label.

## Where the join sits

British practice split the job years ago. The Flood Estimation Handbook (FEH) statistical pathway gives a peak: how rare, how big. A rainfall-runoff method gives a hydrograph, usually a synthetic one. A hydraulic model spreads that hydrograph and draws a map.

Each step is competent. The join is the weak point. The peak does not know the volume. The synthetic hydrograph does not know this storm. The map does not know it was fed a template. You can do each step correctly and still put the water in the wrong street, because the object that carries the storm into the map was never this river.

CEFA makes the hydrograph a catchment estimate, with a band instead of a single line. Tiamat is fast enough to spread the band, not only the middle.

## What is online now

The live public piece is the flow half. [forecast.cameronhann.com](https://forecast.cameronhann.com) takes forecast rainfall for England and Wales and reports a CEFA peak range at the gauges. Experimental. Not a flood warning. For those, use the [Environment Agency service](https://check-for-flooding.service.gov.uk/). It will tell you roughly how big a river's response looks. It is silent on the exact minute of the peak, because timing is the part that is easiest to oversell.

[hydrometric.io](https://hydrometric.io) is the same engine on the design desk: a catchment, a band, a characteristic hydrograph.

Neither site draws inundation yet.

## What goes up next

Tiamat forecasts will be run and put online. What I want on that page is this week's rain, turned into this week's chance, not another 1-in-100 outline.

A low hydrograph, a typical one, a high one. Three Tiamat maps. A cell that is dry in the first and wet in the third is not "the 1-in-100". It is a place the storm can reach, with a measured fraction of the ensemble behind it.

![Schematic of three Tiamat maps from the low, typical and high CEFA hydrographs](/images/articles/the-probabilistic-flood/02-ensemble.jpg)

That is a different product from a planning flood zone. A zone is a policy. A probability updates when the rain forecast does.

Two things have to be true for that to mean anything. There is not a single hydrograph for a given rarity: a short convective peak and a long winter soak at the same return period do not cover the same houses, so Tiamat has to run the band, not the middle. And a flood map is not a photograph of a peak. It is an integral of volume and timing through the terrain. That is why the [hydrograph](/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape) matters, and why [Tiamat](/articles/tiamat) is a physics engine rather than a lookup table.

I am not claiming we now know the future. The 1-in-100 can stay as planning language. What changes is the operational object: a probability for this storm, updated when the rain is, once Tiamat is running on the same forecasts CEFA already is.
