Tiamat is a two-dimensional flood model that runs on NVIDIA GPUs at [7Analytics](https://7analytics.ai), where I am CTO. It takes terrain, river geometry and water (rainfall or a hydrograph) and calculates how that water moves across the floodplain. The output is a georeferenced map of maximum depth. Extent is the wet footprint of that depth.

That physics is not new. Two-dimensional shallow-water models have been the serious way to do this for years. What changed is practical: a GPU can run the same hydraulic calculation across millions of cells at once, which makes an ensemble cheap enough to inspect while the question is still live.

![Illustrative Tiamat maximum-depth map for a 1-in-100-year fluvial run on the Greenbrier, West Virginia, 10 m grid](/images/articles/tiamat/greenbrier-depth-map.png)

That Greenbrier map is a production run, not a render. Ten-metre grid, depth in metres. It is not the historic flood we used for validation. It is what the engine draws when you give it a design event and let the water go.

## Speed, with the limit attached

On a single RTX 5090, a 9-million-cell production run finishes the hydraulic solve in about three minutes. The largest 18-million-cell runs we measured took under ten. Those times are the solve, not the whole job of building the model, and they are not a bake-off against TUFLOW or Flood Modeller. They are what Tiamat did, on hardware we own, on grids we published.

![Measured GPU solve time against model grid size for 23 production runs](/images/articles/tiamat/gpu-performance.png)

A single map gives an answer. An ensemble shows which assumptions control that answer. That is the point of the speed: a second run, and a third, cheap enough that you can still change the question.

Tiamat also keeps channels that are narrower than the grid. A river can carry a lot of water before it spills. Flatten that channel into the terrain and the model floods too much land, too early. Sub-grid channels are how a 10-metre grid still represents in-channel capacity.

## What the hydraulic engine has been checked against

We have compared the engine with observed US floods, not only synthetic tests. Four sites, three kinds of landscape.

![Map of Tiamat historic-validation sites in the United States](/images/articles/tiamat/validation-map.png)

On the Greenbrier in West Virginia the water-level error against 38 surveyed high-water marks was about 25 centimetres. The modelled footprint overlaps the observed flood at roughly 0.75 to 0.85, where 1 is a perfect match. A held-out test on the Little Sioux in Iowa sat around 40 centimetres. Extent checks in Vermont and North Carolina sit in the same range.

![Charts of Tiamat water-level error and inundation-extent overlap against observed floods](/images/articles/tiamat/historic-validation.png)

Those numbers test the engine that spreads water. They do not prove that every rainfall dataset and every runoff assumption will reproduce every real flood. Tiamat's own testing found that distinction: the hydraulics are further ahead than the complete rain-to-flood chain. That work continues.

The metrics use different truth sets and masks. They are evidence coverage, not a league table.

## What it is for

Tiamat is not a website you log into. It is the machinery under 7Analytics: hazard maps, scenario ensembles, and physically consistent examples for models to learn from. Physics first. The customer still sees a depth, a chance, a building. Tiamat is how we can test more places, more storms and more assumptions without hiding where the uncertainty came from.

The hydrograph that feeds it is the other half of the problem. That is [CEFA](/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape). Once Tiamat forecasts are running on the same storms, the two together are a [probability](/articles/the-probabilistic-flood).
