When a flood model needs an inflow, the standard UK route estimates a peak from the Flood Estimation Handbook (FEH) and then applies a synthetic hydrograph. The peak is estimated from data. The shape is a convention: a triangle, or a winter profile, scaled to that peak.

A hydrograph is flow against time. Rise, peak, fall, recession. It is the thing you actually put into the model. Two hydrographs with the same peak can carry very different volumes, arrive on different timescales, and wet different land. The convention is doing more work than it looks.

![Three characteristic hydrographs for the Tame, Kent and Lambourn](/images/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape/00-cover.jpg)

CEFA (Catchment-Event Frequency Analysis) exists because that shape can be treated as a property of the catchment, not as a template.

## Same peak, different flood

CEFA produces a characteristic hydrograph from a catchment's own storm history. Peak-normalised, the three below are the same height on the chart. They are not the same flood.

On the Tame at Bescot, 174 km² of urban West Midlands, the characteristic rise is about two hours. On the Kent above Kendal, similar area, steep and wet, it is about four. On the Lambourn at Shaw, chalk, the recession is still running three days later.

![Peak-normalised CEFA characteristic hydrographs for the Tame, the Kent and the Lambourn](/images/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape/01-signatures.jpg)

The volume under the curve is the useful number. A peak-hour is one hour of flow at the peak rate. The Tame and the Kent carry about 11 of those. The Lambourn carries about 31. Same peak, almost three times the water.

![Volume under the same three hydrographs, peak-normalised](/images/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape/02-volume.jpg)

That is why time-to-peak and hydrograph volume are not decoration around a 1-in-100. They decide whether the floodplain has time to fill. A 2D model given the Kent shape and the Lambourn peak, or the other way around, is answering a different question from the one on the drawing.

![A triangular design hydrograph overlaid on a family of catchment shapes](/images/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape/04-triangle.jpg)

FEH is good at the peak. It was never trying to be a family of historical shapes. If you need to run a hydraulic model you still have to decide when the water arrives and how long it sits. That decision is currently a convention. CEFA makes it a catchment estimate.

## Shapes from the record

The same variation is in the events, not only in catchment type.

Boscastle on 16 August 2004 was a convective flash flood on the Valency: out of bank in the afternoon, back toward bankfull by evening. Hours, not days. Storm Desmond on 5 December 2015 was a long orographic storm on already-wet Cumbrian catchments. The Eden at Sheepmount reached about 1,680 m³/s; the Kent at Sedgwick about 527 m³/s. Summer 2007 on the Severn ran over days. A large minority of events are double-peaked, because the storm was.

![Schematic hydrographs of Boscastle, Storm Desmond, summer 2007 and a double-burst event](/images/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape/03-history.jpg)

Those panels are schematics of event types, not a particular 15-minute gauge trace. They are here because a method that only returns a peak still has to pick one of them. Get that pick wrong and you can have a defensible peak on the wrong day, with the wrong volume, over the wrong houses.

## What CEFA actually returns

CEFA is a storm-response method. It takes the storms that fall on a catchment over decades and estimates the river's response. Not only each year's largest flood. The ordinary storms as well. The output is a range on the peak, and a characteristic hydrograph for that catchment.

That is a different object from FEH pooling, which borrows the shape of the flood-frequency curve from other rivers that look similar on paper. Pooling is useful. Those neighbours are still not this catchment's storm record.

The public version is live. A point on [hydrometric.io](https://hydrometric.io) returns P10, P50 and P90 peaks plus a characteristic hydrograph. The same engine, pointed at forecast rainfall, is the experimental outlook at [forecast.cameronhann.com](https://forecast.cameronhann.com).

![A Cumbrian CEFA hydrograph as a typical line inside a P10 to P90 band](/images/articles/the-flood-is-a-shape/05-band.jpg)

P10, P50 and P90 are the low, typical and high ends of the peak range. In 10 cases out of 100 the peak stays below the bottom line; in 90 it stays below the top. The shape is the catchment's characteristic hydrograph, scaled to those peaks.

It is not yet a new shape for each storm. Storm-conditioned shape is the next product. Today's hydrograph is how this river tends to flood, given its landscape, not how this particular burst will. Antecedent state, the other half of the same programme, is in [What a river remembers](/articles/what-a-river-remembers).

## Limits

The FEH statistical pathway remains the method you can currently defend in a flood risk assessment. CEFA is beta. It is not certified for design. Beyond the 1-in-50, the far tail still uses FEH pooling. That is stated because nobody has the observations to test a 1-in-1000 claim, including ours.

What CEFA does give you is a second view of the same catchment: a band instead of a single peak, and a hydrograph that belongs to the river rather than to the template.

[Tiamat](/articles/tiamat) is the engine that spreads that hydrograph across the land. Once those maps are running on the same storms, the two together are a [probability of inundation](/articles/the-probabilistic-flood).
